I learnt to be strong in the middle of a full life. Strength taught me how to balance all of it.

I’m Salwa.

I’m a Women’s Strength Coach, Strength Educator, Competitive Powerlifter and a mom of two.

For the past 12 years, I’ve built a full-time career coaching women in strength and fitness, educating personal trainers and growing into a leadership role at Canada’s largest fitness company.

My work there was recognized with multiple awards and accolades as I helped build internal and external resources for personal trainers.

At the same time, I raised my family with my husband, lived with my in-laws, trained and competed as a powerlifter, and coached and refereed in the sport.

People often think I started out strong, and that I always loved fitness or that I loved lifting weights.

The truth is, I hated it.

I hated everything about weight training and fitness.

I spent my childhood being told I was overweight and that weight training was something men did, not me. It was a world I never wanted or needed to belong in.

I grew up in a country and culture where being small was a cherished prize. And everyone around me had that prize, so I was always left desiring it.

And if I ever forgot that being thin was the prize, there was always someone out there to remind me that’s what I should be chasing.

Otherwise, I lived an incredibly protected life in a place where girls needed to be protected.

My only exposure to fitness was watching sports with my four brothers. They all loved to play sports, and I got to watch them.

But what happens to a child when they’re not consistently exposed to movement or play?

They may put on weight. But more importantly, they may not develop a mind-muscle connection, baseline strength or the spatial awareness that helps them understand how to move their body.

At 17, I moved to another country. Away from an unsafe environment for girls, I discovered the freedom to move without restriction.

I chose to pursue the one form of movement no one had ever restricted me from: dance.

I loved dancing.

I loved it because I could be any size, any shape and a complete novice, but I could still be free. I loved music, and I could express myself in the safety of my room, alone, where no one was stopping me.

For a few years, I pursued dance because I wanted to get better at it. I loved watching people move, and I loved learning from them.

Then, around the age of 26, as a dance teacher watched me, she told me that improving my skill would require stronger arms and legs.

“Go lift some weights,” she said.

And that’s how I began falling in love with weights.

And as I was falling in love, something else was happening.

I realized that shifting my focus from “How do I lose?” to “How do I gain?” led me, unexpectedly, to the most cherished prize: losing weight.

Then began the real journey.

The shift took me from the dance room to the weight room and eventually into a full-time career coaching women, educating personal trainers and growing into leadership.

Along the way, I taught, powerlifted, coached, refereed, pursued the education I needed to excel in my career, raised a family, maintained relationships and pushed for change within corporate fitness.

I was now in my 30s, and life was incredibly full. But it was also exhausting.

Along the way, I heard a lot:

“Too much weight.”“Too little weight.”“Too skinny.”“Too strong.” “Forget your career. Have more children.”“Forget your children. Have your career.” “You’ll hurt yourself.”“Your uterus will fall out.”

People always have a lot to say. The wise learn to seek help and sort out the noise.

Every step of this journey taught me difficult but important lessons. And I feel that if I don’t share those lessons to help other women shape full but easier lives, then my hardships were for nothing.

Your values, culture and priorities matter.

Growing up across cultures and working with women from different backgrounds, taught me that people have very different relationships with family, food, movement, time, and personal goals. And that in order for them to get stronger, live more fulfilled lives and filter out the noise, I need to work with their values, norms and priorities. I spent my own career navigating how to balance this and helped others do the same.

Building strength while life is happening.

What I learnt is that not every chapter of life can hold the same amount.

For us to have more fulfilled lives, we need support and the strength to do it. We can do this when we understand what, how and when to push forward and pull back.

Not everything needs to be pushed forward at the same time.

Lifting works this way: When a lifter needs to push a weight that their body is truly capable of, they can only do that by prioritizing one lift. In the meantime, they must maintain the other lifts so they don’t lose the progress already made.

Life works best that way too. When life reaches that full stage, with career, fitness, relationships and caregiving responsibilities, holding some things steady, lowering the focus on others and pushing one forward is how we can eventually progress all of them.

But what does maintenance actually look like?

Which areas do we choose to push forward?

What does forward look like?

What is the Minimum Effective Dose to keep progressing them all?

I love lifting because it taught me the secret sauce to balancing life.
